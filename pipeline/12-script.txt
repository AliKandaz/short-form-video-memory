This three-hundred-pound manhole cover just flew fifty feet into the air. It wasn't sewer gas. It was road salt.

Under this street there's no sewer, just a concrete room full of power cables. But in winter, trucks salt the road, and
salty slush drips through the lid onto the cables. So every cable wears a thick coat of insulation. But salt water eats
into it, winter after winter. One day the current breaks through and jumps. This is an arc. It cooks the coat into smoke
and gas, and the room fills up. Then one more spark lights it, and the only way out is up.

In New York, thousands of manholes smoke every year, and a few dozen blow. So the power company swaps solid lids for lids
full of holes, and the gas gets out before it builds up. That's why so many lids in New York are full of holes. But holes
let salt water in too. So sensors below sniff for heat and gas, and raise the alarm. But a big blast can still throw a
lid. So some lids get latches. They lift a little, let the gas out, and settle back.

And the pigeon that was sitting on this one? It's fine. Wire the street, vent the lid, and keep it on the ground.
