Replies ready:
- "Is it radioactive?" → No. The tower's water only cools the condenser, where the plant's steam turns back into water; it never
  touches the reactor or the reactor's water. The cloud is water vapour.
- "So why is it so tall if it's empty?" → The height is the engine. The taller the column of warm, wet air, the harder it pulls
  cool air in at the bottom, with no fans at all.
- "Why the curved shape?" → The double curve stiffens a thin concrete shell, only about 6-7 inches thick at the waist, against
  the wind.
- "How much water goes through it?" → For a big unit, hundreds of thousands of gallons a minute. A few percent evaporates each
  pass and is topped up from the river.
- "What happened in 1965?" → At Ferrybridge C in England on 1 November 1965, a gale knocked down three of the eight 375-foot
  towers within an hour. The towers still standing were strengthened with more reinforced concrete.
- "Why is there a duck?" → He likes the weather.

Facts behind the replies (checked 20 Nov 2026, search summaries; the source pages can't be opened from a session): natural draft
needs no fans (warm, moist air is lighter and rises, drawing air in at the base); the cooling equipment sits in roughly the bottom
10 m and the rest is an empty shell (Designing Buildings); shells 16-18 cm thick at the waist in existing towers (a cooling-tower

reactor (World Nuclear Association).
