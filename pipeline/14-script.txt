Cut open this nuclear cooling tower, and it's pouring rain inside. Warm rain, day and night. Above it, the tower is empty.

This water just cooled the plant's steam. But it came out hot, hundreds of thousands of gallons a minute. So you spray it
over stacks of thin plastic sheets. This is the fill. As it trickles down, a little evaporates and carries the heat away. But
still air soon gets soaked, and the cooling stops. So you build a hollow chimney over the rain. Warm, wet air rises on its
own and pulls cool air in at the base. This is natural draft. But a tube this tall has to stand up to a gale. So you pinch
its waist. That curve keeps a shell about six inches thick standing.

In 1965, a gale in England knocked down three of them in an hour. The rest got thicker walls.

But the rising air carries drops away. So baffles above the sprays catch them. These are drift eliminators. What gets out is
warm, wet air, and the cold turns it into a cloud. That's why it's biggest on cold, damp days. It's just water, and it never
touches the reactor. The only one who loves the weather in here? This duck. Rain it down, pull the air up, and let it drift
away.
