### Facts checked (23 Nov 2026, from web-search summaries; sources in `17-bill.md` and below)
- **T. rex:** Sue is 12.3–12.4 m (40 ft) long and 3.66–3.96 m (12–13 ft) tall at the hips; 8.4–14 t, at least 9 t in a 2011
  model (Wikipedia; PLoS ONE 2011). So his back is level with a one-storey roof edge, and a 3 ft fence is at his ankle.
- **Cable fence:** cable-style fencing is common at American zoos (ZooChat on the Oregon Zoo's Elephant Lands, whose barrier
  is a bollard-and-cable system; a USDA study of dangerous-animal exhibits counts rope, chain or cable barriers at 55
  facilities). Oregon's cables are electrified; ours are not, and the films' electric fence stays out.
- **Ha-ha:** the real name of a sunken barrier, also called a sunk fence: a deep ditch with a wall on one side, so the view
  stays open (Wikipedia; the National Trust; the National Gallery's garden-history lexicon). Zoos use the same idea as dry
  moats (New Zealand's zoo containment standards list them). The script names the thing, not a zoo's word for it.
- **Wallow:** zoos keep mud wallows and pools for elephants and rhinos, to cool off and as enrichment (Taronga, Singapore
  Zoo, Reid Park, Werribee; the AAZK's elephant enrichment guidelines).
- **Night house:** the indoor quarters animals are shut in at night, which also lets keepers clean the yard safely
  (Flamingo Land's giraffes; the National Zoo's elephant keepers; the common US term, per ZooChat).
- **Laminated glass:** zoo windows for big animals are laminated: 3 × 12 mm (about 1.5 in) for lions, as at the Lincoln Park
  Zoo's lion house, and 52 mm (about 2 in) at Bristol Zoo's gorilla house. A US brick is 2 1/4 in high: "as thick as a
  brick".
- **Not in the narration:** a T-rex would eat about 140 kg (300 lb) of meat a day (The Conversation's estimate, via
  FiveThirtyEight); it could go in the YouTube description.
