Pinned comment: Would you stand on the dock? Yes or no. And what do we build next?

Replies ready:
- "What's in the bill?" → A $50 tow strap to start. Then 12 marine piles at about $3,000 each and 200 ft of rail
  ($37,900), one more pile for the trigger ($3,000), 2 tons of used anchor chain ($2,700), 5 tons of pig iron ballast
  ($2,500) and a 25–35 ton boat hoist ($351,000). $397,150 in rough US prices, 2026, before the boat, the cradle, the winch
  and the neighbor's dock.
- "Why didn't the tow rope work?" → A 3-inch strap is sold as "30,000 lb", and that's its breaking strength. A 45 ft steel
  workboat weighs about 40,000 lb.
- "Are drag chains real?" → Yes. Shipyards lay piles of chain along the sides of a hull and tie them on, so it slows down
  once it's in the water and doesn't hit the far bank. That's how ships are launched into narrow rivers.
- "What holds a ship before it's launched?" → A launching trigger, Britannica's name for it: a catch that holds the
  cradle until the moment, then lets it go.
- "Why did it lean?" → A new hull with nothing in it floats high and tips easily. Iron or other heavy weight low in the
  hull, called ballast, lowers its centre of gravity and it sits level.
- "Why not launch it straight in?" → Big yards launch end-on, stern first. Sideways launches are real too, where the
  water is too narrow to run a hull in end first.
- "What's that yellow crane?" → A boat hoist: it rolls out on two piers, straddles a boat, passes slings under the hull
  and lifts it out. You'll often hear it called by a maker's trademark.
